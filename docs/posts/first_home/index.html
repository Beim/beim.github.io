New Zealand Property Guide: Property Types and Ownership

This guide explains the different types of property in New Zealand and the legal ways you can own them.

Part 1: Common Property Types in New Zealand

Property Type describes what you are buying (e.g., a house, an apartment, a townhouse).

  • Standalone House (or Dwelling): This is a freestanding house on its own section of land. It doesn’t share any walls with other properties. This is often considered the traditional “Kiwi dream” and offers the most privacy and space.

  • Townhouse: A townhouse is a multi-level home that shares one or two walls with adjoining properties. They are often found in terraced (row) housing developments. They typically have a small outdoor area and are a popular, more affordable option in urban areas.

  • Apartment: An apartment is a self-contained home within a larger building. Apartments are most common in city centres and offer convenient, low-maintenance living. They often come with access to shared facilities like gyms and swimming pools, and are usually managed by a Body Corporate.

  • Unit (or Flat): This is a single-level dwelling that is part of a larger block or complex. Units are similar to apartments but are typically in smaller, older complexes with fewer amenities. They are often found in suburban areas and can be an economical housing option.

  • Lifestyle Block: This is a larger section of rural land with a house on it, but one that is too small to be a full-scale commercial farm. They are popular with those who want a rural lifestyle with more space for gardens, pets, or hobbies, while still being within commuting distance of a town or city.

  • Bare Land: This refers to a section of land with no buildings on it. People buy bare land with the intention of building a new home on it.

  • Home and Income: This refers to a property that has a main dwelling plus a separate, self-contained flat or unit that can be legally rented out to provide an income stream for the owner.

Part 2: Main Types of Property Ownership in New Zealand

Type of Ownership describes how you legally own the property. Each type comes with different rights, responsibilities, and restrictions.

Information sourced from Settled.govt.nz

  • Freehold (or Fee Simple): This is the most common and simplest type of ownership. If you have a freehold property, you own the land and (generally) anything built on it. However, your use of the property can be restricted by interests such as:

    • Easements: Rights for neighbours or utility providers to use part of your land.
    • Covenants: Rules that limit what you can build or do on the land.
    • Restrictions under the Resource Management Act 1991.
    • Whether the land is Māori freehold land.

  • Leasehold: With leasehold ownership, someone else owns the land, and you purchase the exclusive right to possess the land and buildings for a specific period.

    • You pay ground rent to the landowner.
    • The lease sets out the rent amount, how often it is reviewed, and other expenses.
    • At the end of the lease term, you must return the land and buildings to the owner.

  • Unit Title (or Stratum Estate): This is the most common ownership type for apartments and townhouses. As a unit owner, you own:

    • Your specific apartment or unit, plus any accessory units (garages, etc.).
    • An undivided share of the common property (lifts, lobbies, driveways).
    • You automatically become a member of the Body Corporate, which manages the complex and to which you pay annual levies.

  • Cross-Lease: This is a more complex and older form of ownership. You own two interests:

    1. A share of the freehold title in common with the other leaseholders.
    2. A leasehold interest for the specific building you occupy (usually for 999 years).
    • Changes to the building’s footprint often require the consent of all other cross-leaseholders.

  • Māori Land: This land is subject to the Te Ture Whenua Māori Act 1993 and has a special status. It is typically owned by multiple people, and there are specific rules and a separate court (the Māori Land Court) that governs its use and sale.

Part 3: Relationships Between Property Types and Ownership

The type of building often dictates the most suitable legal ownership structure.

Common Pairings

  • Standalone House -> Freehold (Fee Simple)

    • Why: It’s a single dwelling on a single piece of land. Simple ownership for a simple structure.

  • Apartment / Unit / Townhouse -> Unit Title

    • Why: This structure was created for multi-dwelling complexes. It allows individual ownership within a shared complex managed by a Body Corporate.

  • Older Apartment / Unit / Townhouse -> Cross-Lease

    • Why: Before Unit Titles, cross-leases were a common way to subdivide land for multiple dwellings.

Summary Table

Property Type (The “What”) Most Common Type of Ownership (The “How”) Why it’s the common pairing
Standalone House Freehold / Fee Simple It’s a single dwelling on a single piece of land. Simple ownership for a simple structure.
Apartment / Unit Unit Title Perfect for multi-dwelling buildings. Allows individual ownership within a shared complex.
Townhouse Unit Title or Cross-Lease Unit Title is modern. Cross-Lease is an older method for achieving a similar outcome.
Lifestyle Block Freehold / Fee Simple Similar to a standalone house, just on a much larger piece of land.

Part 4: Common Cladding Types in New Zealand

Cladding is the external covering or skin of a building that protects it from weather while providing insulation and aesthetic appeal.

Main Cladding Types with Pros and Cons

  • Brick: The most popular cladding option in New Zealand. Standard 70mm brick is durable, low maintenance, aesthetically pleasing, and performs well in New Zealand’s climate.

    Pros:

    • Excellent thermal insulation
    • Weather resistant and long-lasting
    • Little to no maintenance required (if unpainted)
    • Traditional and enduring appeal
    • Fire resistant

    Cons:

    • Susceptible to earthquake damage if not properly mounted
    • Time-consuming and labour-intensive installation
    • Higher upfront costs

  • Timber/Weatherboard: Traditional choice, especially popular in villas and bungalows as well as modern homes. Common types include Cedar, Pine, and Larch, each offering distinct appearance and durability.

    Pros:

    • Natural warmth and character
    • Timeless aesthetic appeal
    • Easy installation
    • Flexibility during seismic events
    • Proven durability over decades

    Cons:

    • Vulnerable to moisture, heat, and splitting
    • Requires regular maintenance (painting/staining)
    • Higher ongoing maintenance costs

  • Fibre Cement: Including brands like Harditex, James Hardie, and Weathertex. Made from sand, cement and cellulose fibres, applied as sheets or horizontal boards. Stable, reliable and durable with minimal maintenance requirements.

    Pros:

    • High fire, pest, and rot resistance
    • Strong energy efficiency
    • Minimal maintenance required
    • Available in various textures and finishes
    • Can mimic wood, stone, or brick appearance

    Cons:

    • Brittle nature - poor performance during earthquakes
    • Lacks flexibility of timber
    • Less character than natural materials

  • Metal Cladding: Aluminum options like Nuwall & Ultraclad are growing in popularity, especially for coastal properties due to their maintenance-free nature. Available in various styles, profiles and colors.

    Pros:

    • Modern and creative appearance
    • Fully recyclable
    • Long lifespan (25-40+ years)
    • Low maintenance
    • Wide range of colours and styles

    Cons:

    • Higher upfront costs
    • Aluminum composite panels susceptible to weather damage
    • Can be noisy in heavy rain/hail

  • Monolithic/Plaster (EIFS): Three common materials: plastered polystyrene, plastered fibre cement, or stucco (sand/cement mix). Note: Direct-fix monolithic cladding systems were associated with weathertightness issues during the “Leaky Homes” period (late 1980s to mid-2000s).

    Pros:

    • Modern, seamless aesthetic
    • Uniform and sculpted appearance
    • Contemporary design appeal
    • Improved systems post-2004 with cavity design

    Cons:

    • Known weathertightness issues (especially pre-2004)
    • High risk of water ingress
    • Associated with “Leaky Homes” crisis
    • Requires expert installation

  • Stone/Stone Veneer: Natural stone including sandstone, limestone and schist. Particularly common in Central Otago where schist is sourced locally.

    Pros:

    • Sophisticated and timeless aesthetic
    • Excellent water protection when properly installed
    • Highly durable
    • Low maintenance
    • Suits various architectural styles

    Cons:

    • High material and installation costs
    • Heavy weight requires structural consideration
    • Limited local availability of some stone types

Important Considerations

  • Cladding choice depends on climate, maintenance requirements, budget, aesthetic preferences, and local building codes
  • Direct-fix cladding systems require regular monitoring and maintenance for weathertightness
  • Cavity-based systems generally offer better moisture protection
  • New Zealand building code requires cladding to have a minimum 15-year lifespan

Part 5: Common Roofing Materials in New Zealand

Roofing materials protect the structure from weather while contributing to insulation, aesthetics, and structural integrity.

Main Roofing Materials with Pros and Cons

  • Corrugated Steel: The most popular roofing material in New Zealand, available in various profiles and coatings including Zincalume and Colorsteel.

    Pros:

    • Lightweight - ideal for seismic conditions
    • Long lifespan (40-50+ years with quality coatings)
    • Excellent weather resistance
    • Wide range of colours and profiles
    • Recyclable and environmentally friendly
    • Cost-effective installation
    • Handles building movement well

    Cons:

    • Can be noisy during heavy rain/hail
    • Prone to denting from severe weather or debris
    • Requires proper insulation to prevent condensation
    • May fade over time without quality coatings

  • Concrete Tiles: Heavy-duty roofing offering excellent durability and insulation properties.

    Pros:

    • Exceptional longevity (50+ years)
    • Superior thermal mass and insulation
    • Fire resistant
    • Quiet during rainfall
    • Available in various colours and profiles
    • Low maintenance once installed

    Cons:

    • Very heavy - requires robust structural support
    • Higher installation costs
    • Vulnerable to cracking in earthquakes
    • Individual tiles can break and need replacement
    • More complex installation process

  • Clay Tiles: Traditional terracotta or glazed clay tiles offering classic Mediterranean aesthetics.

    Pros:

    • Extremely durable (100+ year lifespan possible)
    • Excellent thermal properties
    • Fire and pest resistant
    • Distinctive aesthetic appeal
    • Natural and environmentally friendly
    • Fade resistant

    Cons:

    • Very heavy requiring structural reinforcement
    • High material and installation costs
    • Brittle and can crack during earthquakes
    • Limited colour options compared to modern materials
    • Individual tiles may slip or break

  • Membrane Roofing (Butynol/EPDM): Synthetic rubber membranes primarily used on flat or low-pitch roofs.

    Pros:

    • Excellent for flat/low-pitch applications
    • Seamless waterproof barrier
    • Flexible and handles building movement
    • UV resistant with proper coating
    • Relatively cost-effective for flat roofs

    Cons:

    • Limited to low-pitch applications
    • Requires expert installation
    • Vulnerable to punctures and tears
    • Shorter lifespan than hard materials (15-25 years)
    • Ongoing maintenance required

  • Metal Tiles: Steel designed to replicate traditional tile appearance while maintaining metal benefits.

    Pros:

    • Combines metal durability with tile aesthetics
    • Lighter than concrete/clay tiles
    • Good seismic performance
    • Long lifespan with quality coatings
    • Available in various colours and profiles

    Cons:

    • Higher cost than basic corrugated steel
    • Can be noisy in severe weather
    • More complex installation than basic steel
    • May not achieve authentic tile appearance

  • Asphalt Shingles: Less common in New Zealand, mainly found on older homes or specific architectural styles.

    Pros:

    • Cost-effective initial installation
    • Variety of colours and styles
    • Relatively lightweight
    • Easy to replace individual shingles

    Cons:

    • Shorter lifespan (15-25 years)
    • Not ideal for New Zealand’s UV conditions
    • Can blow off in high winds
    • Less environmentally friendly
    • Regular maintenance required

Important Considerations

  • Steel roofing dominates due to New Zealand’s seismic activity and weather conditions
  • Roof pitch determines suitable materials (membrane for flat, tiles/steel for pitched)
  • Coastal locations require enhanced corrosion resistance
  • Building code compliance varies by region and building type
  • Professional installation crucial for warranty and performance
  • Consider long-term maintenance costs, not just initial price
